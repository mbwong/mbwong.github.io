About

I work as an assistant professor in management and strategy at HKU Business School, with a joint appointment in economics, and a courtesy appointment at HKU School of Governance and Policy.

My research focuses on labor markets, firm organization, housing policy, relational contracts, artificial intelligence, inequality, and digital currency.

I am the founding director of Hong Kong Future Economy Institute (HKFEI), an independent public-policy think tank dedicated to evidence-based policy innovation in Hong Kong. I serve on the Housing Authority Subsidised Housing Committee.

I am affiliated with IZA, Rockwool Foundation, HKU Org Econ, HKU CAMO, HKU Stone Centre, HKU CCCW, HKU Jockey Club ESG Research Institute, and HKU Real Estate Lab.

Working Papers

Domestic Outsourcing and Young-worker Entry into the Formal Sector

(with Mayara Felix)

Last Updated: August 2026

Abstract (click to expand): Contract firms often pool demand for non-core work—security, cleaning—that is thin at any one employer. We show that this pooling creates occupation-specific scale that substitutes for worker experience at hiring. In a novel pattern we document for all occupations in Brazil’s linked employer–employee data, hiring of youth who never had a formal sector job rises with employers’ occupation-specific scale. For security guards, 1990s pro-outsourcing reforms increased young-worker entry into the formal sector: a triple-differences design shows guard employment rose by 4% and hiring from unemployment or informality by 7%, reallocating employment toward younger workers at unchanged demographic-adjusted wages.

The Displacement Effects of Domestic Outsourcing

(with Mayara Felix)

Last Updated: July 2026

Abstract (click to expand): Evidence that domestic outsourcing lowers pay comes largely from on-site transfers, in which workers move to a contractor but keep the same jobs. Displacement is rarely observed: whether workers lose their jobs, where they go, how earnings evolve. In Brazil’s 1993–1994 pro-outsourcing reforms, which differentially affected security guards, such transfers were rare; firms instead used occupational layoffs, shedding their guards while keeping other workers. Displaced guards’ employment recovered within five years, but many changed occupations and wages stayed about 12% lower. Lifetime losses average 1.2 to 1.5 years of pre-layoff earnings, concentrated among workers from high-wage firms, reflecting lost premia.

NBER Working Paper 35584

Relational Contracts and the Scope of Intermediary Firms

Last Updated: July 2026

Abstract (click to expand): Why do some intermediaries specialize narrowly while others operate broadly? I study this question in a model where intermediaries choose scope and employ producers they must monitor and coordinate, with all contracts sustained relationally. Broader scope deepens buyer-manager relationships but requires larger scale, diluting monitoring and raising wages. Fees are a multiplicative markup on wages, and markups are higher for infrequent buyers, so the cost of breadth falls hardest on those whose demand is most intermittent. The central prediction reverses the naive one-stop-shop intuition: a buyer whose demand is rare uses a narrow specialist even when her task needs are diversified, because a broad manager's markup grows steeply with infrequency. Scope expansion raises total output but can harm remaining specialist buyers through a pool composition externality that worsens monitoring quality.

Promise and Perils of Platform Money: Evidence from a Barter Community

Last Updated: January 2026

Revise and resubmit, Economic Journal

Abstract (click to expand): This paper analyzes the effects of platform money issuance on user trade activity using microdata from a Toronto-based barter community that introduced a redeemable platform currency and later experienced a crisis. Three unanticipated events provide exogenous variation: a five‑fold increase in token supply, a sharp restriction of redemption opportunities, and a complete halt of redemption. Using event study designs, I show that monetary expansion raised total trades by around 57 percent, entirely via token‑mediated trades, with barter unchanged. Restricting and then halting redemption reduced token acceptance and token‑mediated trade, with the initial restriction also lowering barter, via reductions in platform use and new user entry. The findings highlight how platform money may increase trade, but caution that reputational enforcement may amplify the costs of a redemption crisis.

Redemption Design and Digital Currency Adoption

(with Baiyun Jing, Yang You, and Yulin Zhong)

Last Updated: January 2026

Abstract (click to expand): Many digital currencies are backed by redemption promises, but little evidence or theory informs currency redemption policy. We study the impact of redemption policy on digital currency adoption and flows, leveraging novel geographical variation in redemption convenience and transaction data from a peer-to-peer used goods trading platform. We show in a search-theoretic framework that redeemability may induce digital currency adoption at zero steady-state cost. However, the evidence is more consistent with a heterogeneous-agent model, in which tokens flowed on average towards users who were closer to redemption opportunities and redeemability is both costly and necessary for digital money circulation. A key implication is that optimal policy may impose some degree of redemption friction.

Managerial Coordination in Decentralized Markets

(with Duoxi Li)

Last Updated: July 2026

Economic Journal, forthcoming

Abstract (click to expand): Why do some buyers contract directly with producers, while others contract via an intermediary that coordinates assignments and monitors performance? We develop a repeated-game model in which intermediaries aggregate fluctuating demand, lengthening productive relationships and making cooperation easier to sustain. This benefit comes at the cost of double marginalization: an added layer of rents is needed to motivate the intermediary. The trade-off determines which buyers rely on an intermediary and which contract directly. In equilibrium, producers working through intermediaries earn lower, less variable pay but greater job security. Intermediaries expand trade, yet shift its gains from producers to buyers. Managerial coordination also promotes specialization. The framework sheds new light on professional service firms, global sourcing intermediaries, and digital matching platforms.

Domestic Outsourcing and Employment Security

(with Naijia Guo and Duoxi Li)

Journal of Development Economics, June 2026

Abstract (click to expand): Domestic outsourcing is known to reduce worker wages, but its effect on employment security—a key dimension of job quality—has not been studied. Using Brazil’s comprehensive employee-employer linked data, we find that outsourcing reduces exit from formal employment among cleaners and security guards during their first few years of tenure. The observed reduction in employment hazard is larger in cities with greater volatility in labor demand. The reduction is not attributable to differences in worker characteristics or differential exposure to local labor market shocks. The estimates suggest that outsourcing had larger positive effects on the net present value of worker earnings than implied by wage differentials alone. The patterns are consistent with a search-theoretic model in which outsourcing eases reassignment across firms.

Subsidize Rent or Subsidize Homeownership? Evidence from a Hong Kong Experiment

Last Updated: June 2026

Regional Science and Urban Economics, forthcoming

Abstract (click to expand): Governments often provide homeownership subsidies, but their effects may depend critically on institutional context. This paper estimates the impacts of Hong Kong’s Tenants Purchase Scheme, an unusual program that allowed 183,700 regularly income-tested public housing tenants to buy restricted ownership rights. Leveraging its incomplete roll-out between 1998 and 2006, I find that the program increased the share of "well-off" households in treated housing estates by 44 percent from 11 p.p. Further evidence suggests that incumbent households purchased units to avoid regular income tests and reconfigured co-residence among family members to obtain additional public housing units. The unintended consequence was to exacerbate hardship for excluded low-income populations.

Ideological Bias and Trust in Information Sources

(with Matthew Gentzkow and Allen T. Zhang)

American Economic Journal: Microeconomics, May 2025

Abstract (click to expand): We study the role of endogenous trust in amplifying ideological bias. Agents in our model learn a sequence of states from sources whose accuracy is ex ante uncertain. Agents learn these accuracies by comparing their own reasoning about the states based on introspection or direct experience to the sources' reports. Small biases in this reasoning can cause large ideological differences in the agents' trust in information sources and their beliefs about the states, and may lead agents to become overconfident in their own reasoning. Disagreements can be similar in magnitude whether agents see only ideologically aligned sources or diverse sources.
